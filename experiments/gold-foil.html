The physics behind the flashes

Nothing here is scripted — the scattering angles come straight from Coulomb's law.

/ 01

Two positive charges repel

An α-particle carries charge +2e. A gold nucleus carries +79e. As the α-particle approaches a nucleus, the Coulomb force pushes it away, bending its path into a hyperbola.

F = k·q₁q₂ / r²
/ 02

The impact parameter sets the angle

How close the α-particle's aim passes to the nucleus — the impact parameter b — decides the scattering angle θ. A near head-on hit gives a large deflection.

tan(θ/2) = r₀′ / b
/ 03

Why it is so rare

A nucleus is about 10,000 times smaller than an atom. Almost every α-particle crosses the whole foil without ever getting close to a nucleus — so the striking events are rare.

σ(>θ) = π·r₀′²·cot²(θ/2)
/ 04

The famous law

The number scattered at an angle falls off as 1/sin⁴(θ/2). The histogram you are filling should trace exactly this curve — that agreement is the evidence for a tiny, heavy, positively-charged nucleus.

dσ/dΩ ∝ 1 / sin⁴(θ/2)

What the experiment forces you to conclude

If the positive charge and mass of an atom were spread evenly through it — as Thomson's "plum-pudding" model imagined — the electric field would be far too weak to throw a fast α-particle back. Every particle would pass through with only a tiny nudge.

The fact that a few are deflected through large angles, and occasionally reversed, means the entire positive charge and almost all the mass must be concentrated in a minute central core — the nucleus — around which the electrons sit in mostly empty space. That is the model you have just measured your way to.
